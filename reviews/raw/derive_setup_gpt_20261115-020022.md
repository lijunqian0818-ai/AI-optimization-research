## Theorem 1: proved, including the stated constants

Write
\[
Y_t=\mu_t-X_t.
\]
By A1–A2,
\[
\mathbb E[Y_t\mid\mathcal F_t]=0,\qquad
Y_t\in[\mu_t-n_t,\mu_t].
\]
Thus, conditionally on \(\mathcal F_t\), the range of \(Y_t\) has length \(n_t\).

### 1. Conditional exponential bound

For every real \(\lambda\),
\[
\mathbb E[e^{\lambda Y_t}\mid\mathcal F_t]
\le \exp(\lambda^2n_t^2/8).
\tag{3}
\]

Here is a derivation of the constant. For a centered bounded variable \(W\) supported on an interval of length \(a\), put
\[
g(\lambda)=\log\mathbb E e^{\lambda W}.
\]
Under exponential tilting, \(g''(\lambda)\) is a variance. Every distribution supported on an interval of length \(a\) has variance at most \(a^2/4\): subtract the interval midpoint and use
\[
\operatorname{Var}(W)\le \mathbb E[(W-\text{midpoint})^2]\le a^2/4.
\]
Consequently, \(g''(\lambda)\le a^2/4\). Since \(g(0)=g'(0)=0\), integration gives
\[
g(\lambda)\le \lambda^2a^2/8.
\]
The same argument applied to conditional expectations proves (3). When \(n_t=0\), \(X_t=\mu_t=0\), so (3) also holds.

### 2. Each lane admits an exponential supermartingale

Fix a lane \(j\), and enumerate its positive day indices as
\[
t_1<t_2<\cdots,\qquad t_{m+1}=t_m+d.
\]
Define
\[
S_m=\sum_{r=1}^mY_{t_r},\qquad
V_m=\sum_{r=1}^m n_{t_r}^2,
\]
with \(S_0=V_0=0\), and use the filtration
\[
\mathcal G_m=\mathcal F_{t_{m+1}},\qquad m\ge0.
\]

A3 makes \(Y_{t_m}\) measurable with respect to \(\mathcal G_m\), while \(n_{t_m}\) is measurable with respect to
\(\mathcal G_{m-1}=\mathcal F_{t_m}\). Therefore (3) shows that, for each fixed \(\lambda\in\mathbb R\),
\[
Z_m(\lambda)=\exp\!\left(\lambda S_m-\frac{\lambda^2V_m}{8}\right)
\]
is a nonnegative supermartingale with \(Z_0(\lambda)=1\).

This is the precise reason unrestricted information arriving between adjacent days causes no problem: consecutive observations *within a lane* are separated by \(d\) days.

For completeness, a nonnegative supermartingale starting at one satisfies
\[
\Pr\{\exists m:Z_m\ge e^L\}\le e^{-L}.
\tag{4}
\]
Indeed, stop at the first crossing or a finite horizon. The stopped expectation is at most one, while it is at least \(e^L\) times the probability of crossing by that horizon. Let the horizon increase.

### 3. The linear boundaries give exactly \(b^*\)

For each integer \(k\ge0\), set
\[
a_k=2^k,\qquad
\lambda_k=2\sqrt{L_k/a_k}.
\]
By (4),
\[
\Pr\left\{\exists m:
S_m\ge \frac{L_k}{\lambda_k}+\frac{\lambda_kV_m}{8}
\right\}\le e^{-L_k}.
\]
The boundary is
\[
\frac{L_k}{\lambda_k}+\frac{\lambda_kq}{8}
=
\frac12\sqrt{2^kL_k}
\left(1+\frac{q}{2^{k+1}}\right)
=:f_k(q).
\tag{5}
\]
Applying the same argument to \(-S_m\) gives the corresponding lower-tail bound.

The error allocated to one sign, over every lane and every \(k\), is
\[
\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{-L_k}
=
d\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\frac{\delta}{2dc_0(k+1)^2}
=\frac{\delta}{2}.
\]

Let \(E_\delta^{\rm up}\) be the event that every positive-sign boundary holds, and \(E_\delta^{\rm low}\) the analogous negative-sign event. Then
\[
\Pr(E_\delta^{\rm up})\ge1-\delta/2,\qquad
\Pr(E_\delta^{\rm low})\ge1-\delta/2.
\]
Their intersection \(E_\delta\) has probability at least \(1-\delta\).

On this intersection, for every lane and every partial sum with \(V_m>0\),
\[
|S_m|\le\inf_{k\ge0}f_k(V_m)=b^*(V_m).
\]
The infimum is a minimum because \(f_k(q)\to\infty\) as \(k\to\infty\). If \(V_m=0\), every included \(n_t\) is zero, and hence \(S_m=0=b^*(0)\).

Every lane sum through an arbitrary calendar day \(T\) is one of these partial sums. This proves the first inequality in (1), simultaneously over all \(T\).

### 4. Comparison with \(b\)

For \(q\ge1\), choose \(k=\lfloor\log_2q\rfloor\). Then
\[
2^k\le q<2^{k+1},
\]
so
\[
b^*(q)\le f_k(q)
=\frac12\sqrt{2^kL_k}
\left(1+\frac{q}{2^{k+1}}\right)
\le \sqrt{2^kL_k}=b(q).
\]
The assertion also holds at zero. Thus the factor \(2^{k(q)}\) in the proposed \(b(q)\) is indeed correct.

### 5. Aggregation and observable intervals

Summing the lane inequalities yields, on \(E_\delta\),
\[
\left|\sum_{t\le T}\mu_t-\sum_{t\le T}X_t\right|
\le B_T^*\le B_T
\tag{6}
\]
simultaneously for all \(T\).

By accounting alone,
\[
K_T\le \sum_{t\le T}X_t\le K_T+P_T.
\tag{7}
\]
Combining (6)–(7), dividing by \(N_T>0\), and using
\(0\le\sum_t\mu_t/N_T\le1\) gives (b), and hence (a).

On \(E_\delta^{\rm up}\), summing only the positive-sign inequalities gives
\[
\sum_{t\le T}\mu_t\le K_T+P_T+B_T^*,
\]
which proves the upper bounds. On \(E_\delta^{\rm low}\), the negative-sign inequalities give the lower bounds. This proves (c).

No independence of labels or of revelation times was used.

## Corollary 1.1: proved

Fix \(T\) with \(N_T>0\), and abbreviate
\[
V_T=\sum_{t\le T}n_t^2.
\]
Since \(0\le n_t\le M\),
\[
V_T\le M\sum_{t\le T}n_t=MN_T.
\tag{8}
\]

For any nonzero lane clock \(q=Q_{j,T}\), its epoch index satisfies
\[
k=\lfloor\log_2q\rfloor\le\log_2(MN_T).
\]
Therefore
\[
L_k
=\log(2dc_0/\delta)+2\log(k+1)
\le\ell_T.
\]
As \(2^k\le q\),
\[
b(q)^2=2^kL_k\le q\ell_T.
\]
This also holds at \(q=0\).

Cauchy–Schwarz now gives
\[
B_T
\le\sqrt{\ell_T}\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\sqrt{Q_{j,T}}
\le\sqrt{d\ell_T\sum_jQ_{j,T}}
=\sqrt{dV_T\ell_T}.
\]
Using (8) proves
\[
B_T^*\le B_T\le\sqrt{dV_T\ell_T}
\le\sqrt{dMN_T\ell_T}.
\]

Dividing by \(N_T\) proves the stated sampling radius. Before clipping to \([0,1]\), the interval width is
\[
\frac{P_T+2B_T}{N_T}.
\]
Clipping cannot increase it, proving the width bound.

The sampling term vanishes when \(dM\ell_T/N_T\to0\). Vanishing of the *total* width additionally requires \(P_T/N_T\to0\).

## Corollary 1.2: proved with an assumption clarification

The deterministic bracket is correct under A4 alone. Among the \(P_T\) unresolved binary labels, the number of losses is between zero and \(P_T\). Thus
\[
K_T\le\sum_{t\le T}X_t\le K_T+P_T,
\]
and division by \(N_T\) proves the bracket for \(R_T\).

The second assertion,
\[
|r_T^{\rm entry}-R_T|\le B_T^*/N_T
\quad\text{on }E_\delta,
\tag{9}
\]
uses A1–A3 and Theorem 1. It is not an A4-only concentration result. Under those assumptions, (9) follows immediately from (6).

**Corrected assumption line:** “A4 for the deterministic bracket; A1–A3 for the simultaneous probability-\(1-\delta\) comparison with \(r_T^{\rm entry}\).”

The caution about matured-only rates is justified, but such rates need not always be biased. For an explicit example showing the possible failure, take two entries, one with label zero and one with label one, and reveal only the losing label at the monitoring time. Then
\[
N_T=2,\quad K_T=1,\quad P_T=1,\quad
R_T=\tfrac12,
\]
whereas the matured-only rate is \(1\). Revealing the other label later can satisfy any suitably chosen bounded lag.

## Corollary 1.3: the interval is valid; the identification interpretation needs changes

Use observable accounting for both deadline-label processes. For the stated interpretation of \(K_T^{\rm hi}-K_T^{\rm lo}\), the two labels should be recorded as a **pair**, using a common resolved set. Early information about just one component can be retained separately, but it should not be used to define this paired count.

### 1. Ordering of the targets

By the assumed pointwise ordering,
\[
X_t^{\rm lo}\le X_t^{\rm liq}\le X_t^{\rm hi}.
\]
Conditional expectation preserves inequalities, giving
\[
\mu_t^{\rm lo}\le\mu_t^{\rm liq}\le\mu_t^{\rm hi}.
\]
Consequently,
\[
r_T^{\rm lo}\le r_T^{\rm liq}\le r_T^{\rm hi}.
\tag{10}
\]

### 2. The original radius needs no extra factor of two

Apply the **lower-sided** event from Theorem 1(c) to the low-label process, at error probability \(\delta/2\):
\[
r_T^{\rm lo}\ge
\max\left\{0,\frac{K_T^{\rm lo}-B_T^*}{N_T}\right\}
\quad\text{for all }T.
\]
Apply the **upper-sided** event to the high-label process, also at error probability \(\delta/2\):
\[
r_T^{\rm hi}\le
\min\left\{1,\frac{K_T^{\rm hi}+P_T+B_T^*}{N_T}\right\}
\quad\text{for all }T.
\]
Both processes have the same \(n_t\), lane clocks, and lag \(d_D\), hence the same radius.

A union bound makes these events simultaneous with probability at least \(1-\delta\). Combining them with (10) proves the displayed liquidation interval.

There is no need to apply two two-sided confidence sequences, which would unnecessarily change the logarithmic constant.

### 3. The stated finite-sample width lower bound is correct

Let the displayed endpoints be \(L_T,U_T\). Since \(B_T^*\ge0\),
\[
L_T\le K_T^{\rm lo}/N_T,
\qquad
U_T\ge K_T^{\rm hi}/N_T.
\]
Therefore, pathwise,
\[
U_T-L_T\ge
\frac{K_T^{\rm hi}-K_T^{\rm lo}}{N_T}.
\tag{11}
\]

With paired revelation,
\[
K_T^{\rm hi}-K_T^{\rm lo}
=
\#\{\text{resolved deadline pairs still unsold at their deadline}\}.
\]
The denominator is **all entries**, not only resolved entries.

### 4. “This part does not shrink with more data” is false without an additional assumption

Consider one entry each day. Suppose only the first entry misses its deadline; every later entry is sold by its deadline without loss. Make everything deterministic, and reveal deadline labels at the specified bounded lag.

Eventually,
\[
K_T^{\rm hi}-K_T^{\rm lo}=1,
\qquad N_T=T.
\]
Thus the supposed nonshrinking component is \(1/T\to0\). Moreover, \(P_T\) stays bounded and Corollary 1.1 gives \(B_T^*/T\to0\), so the displayed interval itself shrinks to zero width.

**Corrected persistence statement:** if
\[
\liminf_{T\to\infty}
\frac{K_T^{\rm hi}-K_T^{\rm lo}}{N_T}
\ge\rho>0,
\]
then (11) implies
\[
\liminf_{T\to\infty}(U_T-L_T)\ge\rho.
\]

### 5. The relevant identification limitation is conditional on what data are retained

Writing
\[
U_{ti}^{D}=\mathbf1\{\text{not sold by its deadline}\},
\]
the target-level bracket has exact gap
\[
r_T^{\rm hi}-r_T^{\rm lo}
=
\frac{\sum_{t\le T}
\mathbb E[\sum_iA_{ti}U_{ti}^{D}\mid\mathcal F_t]}
{N_T}.
\tag{12}
\]
This is a conditional expected deadline-unsold fraction. It is not generally equal to the observed fraction in (11).

Deadline information alone can leave liquidation losses unidentified: an unsold position can have the same history through its deadline in two environments, then eventually sell at a gain in one and at a loss in the other. Both completions obey the same deadline labels.

However, eventual liquidation observations can distinguish those completions. Thus the deadline fraction is a limitation of the **deadline-based bracket**, not necessarily an irreducible limitation after all future liquidation information is available.

These changes preserve the confidence interval and its constants.

## Proposition 2: proved with the stated constants

Let
\[
a=1-2\delta>0,\qquad
h=\frac{\sqrt3}{8}\frac{a}{\sqrt B},
\qquad p_\pm=\frac12\pm h.
\]

### 1. The block construction satisfies the assumptions

Selections are deterministic, with \(n_t=M\), so A1 holds. Every selected label in block \(b\) equals \(Z_b\), giving A2.

To specify the infinite-time setup completely, extend the construction beyond \(T=Bd\) by taking no further entries and revealing all \(Z_1,\ldots,Z_B\) at day \(T+1\).

For a day \(t\) in block \(b\), day \(t+d\) is in the next block or after the experiment. Its information therefore includes \(Z_b\). Hence
\[
X_t=MZ_b\in\mathcal F_{t+d},
\]
which proves A3. Revealing each block’s labels at the beginning of the next block provides A4.

Within block \(b\), \(Z_b\) is independent of \(\mathcal F_t\), so
\[
\mu_t=Mp,\qquad
r_T^{\rm entry}=\frac{TMp}{TM}=p.
\]

### 2. Bound the distance between the two experiments

The independent auxiliary variable \(U\) has the same law under both parameters and contributes no divergence. Independence of the blocks gives
\[
D_{\rm KL}(\mathbb P_{p_-}\Vert\mathbb P_{p_+})
=B\,\operatorname{kl}(p_-,p_+).
\]
By symmetry,
\[
\operatorname{kl}(p_-,p_+)
=2h\log\frac{1+2h}{1-2h}.
\]
For \(0\le x<1\),
\[
\log\frac{1+x}{1-x}
=2\int_0^x\frac{du}{1-u^2}
\le\frac{2x}{1-x^2}.
\]
Since \(h<1/4\),
\[
\operatorname{kl}(p_-,p_+)
\le\frac{8h^2}{1-4h^2}
\le\frac{32}{3}h^2.
\]
Therefore
\[
D_{\rm KL}(\mathbb P_{p_-}\Vert\mathbb P_{p_+})
\le\frac{32}{3}Bh^2
=\frac{a^2}{2}.
\]
Pinsker’s inequality yields
\[
\operatorname{TV}(\mathbb P_{p_-},\mathbb P_{p_+})
\le\sqrt{D_{\rm KL}/2}\le a/2.
\tag{13}
\]

The needed Pinsker inequality can also be justified directly here. For the event \(A\) where the first density exceeds the second, let \(x=P(A)\), \(y=Q(A)\); then \(x-y=\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)\). Convexity of \(z\log z\), applied separately on \(A\) and \(A^c\), gives
\[
D_{\rm KL}(P\Vert Q)\ge\operatorname{kl}(x,y).
\]
For fixed \(y\), the latter function has value and first derivative zero at \(x=y\), and second derivative
\[
\frac1{x(1-x)}\ge4.
\]
Integrating twice gives \(\operatorname{kl}(x,y)\ge2(x-y)^2\), proving the inequality used in (13).

### 3. Honesty forces a wide interval with positive probability

Define
\[
A_-=\{\ell\le p_-\le u\},\qquad
A_+=\{\ell\le p_+\le u\}.
\]
Honesty gives
\[
\mathbb P_{p_-}(A_-)\ge1-\delta,\qquad
\mathbb P_{p_+}(A_+)\ge1-\delta.
\]
Using (13) to transfer the second probability,
\[
\mathbb P_{p_-}(A_+)\ge1-\delta-a/2.
\]
Hence
\[
\mathbb P_{p_-}(A_-\cap A_+)
\ge1-2\delta-a/2=a/2.
\]
Interchanging the two laws gives the same lower bound under \(\mathbb P_{p_+}\).

On \(A_-\cap A_+\),
\[
u-\ell\ge p_+-p_-=2h
=\frac{\sqrt3}{4}(1-2\delta)\sqrt{\frac{Md}{N}}.
\]
This proves the claimed probability bound under both laws.

Because the width is nonnegative,
\[
\mathbb E_p[u-\ell]
\ge(2h)(a/2)
=\frac{\sqrt3}{8}(1-2\delta)^2
\sqrt{\frac{Md}{N}},
\]
as claimed.

### 4. Variance calculation

Each block contributes \(Md(Z_b-p)\), so
\[
\sum_{t\le T}(X_t-\mu_t)
=Md\sum_{b=1}^B(Z_b-p).
\]
Therefore
\[
\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\sum_{t\le T}(X_t-\mu_t)\right)
=(Md)^2Bp(1-p)
=MdN\,p(1-p).
\]
For \(N\) independent Bernoulli labels the corresponding variance is \(Np(1-p)\).

Thus the worst-case effective sample size and the polynomial dependence on \(Md/N\) are established. The stated caveats about logarithmic factors and richer filtrations are appropriate.

## Lemma 3: proved

For each period,
\[
\begin{aligned}
h_t^G-h_t^B
&=e_t^Gw_t^G-e_t^Bw_t^B\\
&=(e_t^G-e_t^B)w_t^B
+e_t^G(w_t^G-w_t^B).
\end{aligned}
\]
Taking inner products with \(x_t\), summing over periods, and subtracting \(C^G-C^B\) proves (2).

For the reference \(h_t^E=e_t^Gw_t^B\),
\[
h_t^G-h_t^E=e_t^G(w_t^G-w_t^B),
\]
and
\[
h_t^E-h_t^B=(e_t^G-e_t^B)w_t^B.
\]
These prove the gross assertions. Subtracting the relevant cost differences proves both net assertions.

The qualifications follow as stated, with one wording clarification:

1. If \(e_t^B=0\), then \(h_t^B=0\). Replacing \(w_t^B\) by \(w_t^B+v\) increases the exposure contribution by \(e_t^Gv^\top x_t\) and decreases the composition contribution by exactly the same amount. The total is unchanged. If \(e_t^G=0\), \(w_t^G\) drops out entirely.

2. The net comparison includes the cost difference by definition. An accounting identity supplies no model for \(C^E\).

3. The composition term uses the entire vector \(w_t^G-w_t^B\); nothing in its formula separates selection from factor exposures.

4. The algebra imposes no trading or funding constraints on \(h^E\).

5. Let \(A,C\) denote the displayed exposure and composition terms, and \(A',C'\) their alternative-reference versions. With
   \[
   I=\sum_t(e_t^G-e_t^B)(w_t^G-w_t^B)^\top x_t,
   \]
   direct subtraction gives
   \[
   A'=A+I,\qquad C'=C-I.
   \]
   Thus the **component allocations** differ by the interaction; the totals satisfy \(A+C=A'+C'\). The original version allocates the interaction to composition.

6. Nonlinear performance functionals do not generally distribute over an additive decomposition. For example, compounding two period returns produces \(a+b+ab\); the cross term prevents a general additive attribution inherited from (2).

7. All equalities are pathwise algebra. They introduce no intervention or counterfactual assumptions and hence establish no causal identification.

## Remark 4: the impossibility is proved; the uniqueness sentence is too strong

### What the all-loss environment proves

Take an environment in which every selected label equals one. Then, pathwise,
\[
X_t=n_t.
\]
Since \(n_t\) is \(\mathcal F_t\)-measurable,
\[
\mu_t=\mathbb E[n_t\mid\mathcal F_t]=n_t.
\]
Consequently, whenever \(N_T>0\),
\[
R_T=r_T^{\rm entry}=1.
\]
For every \(\alpha<1\),
\[
\Pr\{N_T>0,\ r_T^{\rm entry}\le\alpha\}
=
\Pr\{N_T>0,\ R_T\le\alpha\}=0.
\tag{14}
\]

Thus no policy can guarantee positive-probability successful trading **in every admissible environment**. In particular, no policy can guarantee both positive trading probability in every environment and almost-sure risk control whenever it trades.

### What does not follow

The sentence

> “The only policy with a universal guarantee is the one that never trades.”

is not established by (14).

If “universal guarantee” includes positive trading probability in every environment, then **no policy** has it: the never-trade policy also fails the positive-trading requirement.

If it means only safety conditional on trading, (14) proves that a universally safe policy must abstain in the all-loss environment. It does not prove that the policy must abstain in every other environment. Such a uniqueness assertion would require an additional argument about what information is available and which environments are observationally indistinguishable. A1–A4 impose no general prohibition on informative or even fully revealing entry-time information.

**Corrected statement:**

> For \(\alpha<1\), no selection policy can guarantee positive-probability trading with \(r_T^{\rm entry}\le\alpha\), or with \(R_T\le\alpha\), in every environment satisfying A1–A4. Any policy that guarantees almost-sure safety whenever it trades must abstain almost surely in the all-loss environment.

The proof is exactly (14). This supports the stated need for environmental restrictions or feasibility assumptions, without asserting uniqueness of the never-trade policy.
